Why infinitely many red circles

Start with the question

An isosceles triangle has side lengths in the ratio 3 : 3 : 2. The two equal sides correspond to the two 3s.

The blue incircle touches all three triangle sides and has area 3 square units.

Above it is an infinite stack of red circles. Every red circle touches both sloping sides and the circle immediately below it.

Find the total area of all the red circles together.

Blue circle One circle. Its AREA is 3, given in the question.
Red circles Infinitely many, with sizes we must prove.
3 : 3 : 2 These are SIDE-LENGTH ratios, not areas and not radii.
What we need to discover: the ratio between neighbouring radii. Once that is proved, the infinite area becomes manageable.

Essential geometry rules — before we start

Isosceles triangles

The altitude from the top vertex to the base is perpendicular to the base and splits it into equal halves. It also bisects the top angle.

Sine in a right triangle

sin(angle) = opposite ÷ hypotenuse. We will use this twice, with the same angle α.

Circle tangent to a line

A radius from the centre to a tangent side is perpendicular to that side, giving a right angle.

Two tangent circles

When circles touch externally, the distance between their centres is the sum of their radii.

Area scale factor

Scaling every length by ½ scales every area by (½)² = ¼.

An infinite series

For a geometric sequence whose common ratio is between −1 and 1, its total is finite. We will prove the total in two ways.

1

Write the given side ratio as actual expressions

Call the triangle’s top vertex A and its bottom corners B and C.

The question says the two equal sides and the base are in the ratio 3 : 3 : 2. That means we can represent their lengths by

AB = 3k,   AC = 3k,   BC = 2k

What is k? It is simply some positive length that we do not know. If k were 5, the sides would be 15, 15 and 10; if k were 7, they would be 21, 21 and 14. Both have the same shape.

Draw the altitude from A to the base, meeting BC at F. Because the triangle is isosceles, F is the midpoint of BC and AF is perpendicular to BC.

FC = BC2 = 2k2 = k
Source of each number: the 3 and 2 come from the stated side ratio. Dividing 2k by 2 is justified because F is the midpoint.
2

Use sine to discover one crucial fraction

Focus on right triangle AFC, the right-hand half of the big isosceles triangle.

Call the angle between the vertical line AF and the sloping side AC α (Greek letter alpha).

Relative to angle α, the opposite side is FC, of length k. The hypotenuse is AC, of length 3k.

sin α = oppositehypotenuse = FCAC = k3k

Divide the top and bottom of the fraction by k. Because k is not zero, the k’s cancel:

sin α = 13

We do not need to calculate the angle α itself. Knowing its sine is one-third is much more useful.

This is the exact same sine = opposite / hypotenuse method you already know. No tangent or advanced trigonometry is needed.
3

Why the blue centre is exactly three radii below A

Let O be the centre of the blue circle, and let R be its radius. Do not confuse R with its given area.

The blue circle touches both equal sloping sides. Therefore its centre O is on the internal angle bisector, the same vertical line AF.

Let E be the point where the blue circle touches the right sloping side AC. The radius OE is perpendicular to AC, so triangle AEO is right-angled at E.

Now use the same angle α at A. In right triangle AEO, the opposite side is OE = R and the hypotenuse is AO.

sin α = OEAO = RAO

But Step 2 already proved sin α = 1/3. These are two expressions for the same trigonometric value, so

RAO = 13

Cross-multiply: 3R = AO. Thus:

AO = 3R

Now call the centre of the first red circle P, and its radius r. Draw its perpendicular radius PD to the same sloping side. By exactly the same right-triangle argument,

AP = 3r
What this means: for every circle that touches both sides, its distance from the apex is precisely three times its own radius. We have proved it; it was not given.
4

Find the ratio of neighbouring radii — rigorously

We have proved that AO = 3R and AP = 3r, where O is the blue centre and P is the first red centre.

Both centres lie on the same straight vertical line, with P between A and O. Therefore:

PO = AO − AP = 3R − 3r

But the blue and red circles are externally tangent. Let T be their touching point. We travel from P to T through one red radius, then from T to O through one blue radius.

PT = r    and    TO = R
PO = PT + TO = r + R

Here is the key connection. The two results above are both the length of the same segment PO. We must therefore make them equal:

3R − 3r = R + r

Add 3r to both sides:

3R = R + 4r

Subtract R from both sides:

2R = 4r

Divide both sides by 4:

r = R2
The first red circle is half the BLUE radius. Because every neighbouring pair has exactly the same kind of tangency and touches the same sloping sides, the argument repeats forever.
5

Half a radius does NOT mean half an area

The area formula for a circle of radius R is πR².

If we replace its radius with R/2, the new area is

π × (R2)² = π × R²4 = πR²4

Compare that with the original area πR². The new area is one-quarter as large:

radius × 12  ⇒  area × 14

We just proved that every red circle has half the radius of the circle below it. Therefore every successive red area is one-quarter of the previous area.

The number 4 is not supplied in the question. It comes from squaring the length scale: 2² = 4.
6

Work out the first few RED areas — where does the 3 come from?

The original question tells us that the blue circle has area 3 square units. That is where the number 3 comes from. It is not the blue radius.

We now know that each next circle has one-quarter of the area of the circle immediately below it.

Blue circle (given)
3
First red circle
3 × 14 = 34
Second red circle
34 × 14 = 316
Third red circle
316 × 14 = 364

And the pattern continues forever. Therefore the total red area is

S = 34 + 316 + 364 + ···

Here we have introduced the letter S to mean all red areas added together. We have NOT yet found the value of S.

To be completely clear: 3 = GIVEN blue area; ¼ = PROVED area multiplier; S = UNKNOWN red total.
7

The visual trick: shrink the ENTIRE stack onto the reds

The entire stack contains the blue circle and every red circle. Its total area is therefore

whole-stack area = 3 + S

We know a half-size copy of a circle has one-quarter of its area. Now imagine shrinking the entire stack about the top vertex A by a length scale of one-half.

The blue circle shrinks into the first red circle. The first red circle shrinks into the second red circle. The second red shrinks into the third red. This happens to every circle in the infinite chain.

So the scaled copy of the whole stack is precisely the original RED stack. The circles cover exactly the same red region, not some additional region.

Area before shrinking
3 + S
Area after shrinking
3 + S4

But the shrunk version is the red stack. Its area is S, by our definition.

S = 3 + S4
Where did this equation come from? LEFT = red-stack area, because we named it S. RIGHT = the whole-stack area (3+S) shrunk to one-quarter. They are equal because shrinking the whole stack produces exactly the red stack.
8

Solve the equation without skipping any algebra

We have just established

S = 3 + S4

Multiply both sides by 4. On the right, the 4 cancels the denominator:

4S = 3 + S

Subtract S from both sides:

4S − S = 3 + S − S
3S = 3

Divide both sides by 3:

S = 1 square unit

This is the sum of ALL red circles, infinitely many of them. It is not the first red circle alone. The first red circle is ¾ square unit, and the remaining infinitely many circles contribute the final ¼.

Meaning of every symbol in the equation: S is the desired total, 3 is the given blue area, and 4 is the area reduction from half-scale lengths.
9

Independent check without shrinking any diagrams

Start with the red-area series from Step 6:

S = 34 + 316 + 364 + ···

One-quarter of S shifts every term one place to the right:

S4 = 316 + 364 + 3256 + ···

Subtract the second line from the first. Every term after the very first matches and cancels:

S − S4 = 34

Write S as four quarters of S:

4S4 − S4 = 34
3S4 = 34
S = 1

The same answer has been obtained in a completely different way.

Why the infinite answer is exact — not an approximation

Check what happens if we stop after a few red circles. Each total gets closer to 1.

For the first N red circles, the exact partial sum is

SN = 1 − 14N

As N grows, 4N becomes arbitrarily large, so 1/4N becomes arbitrarily close to zero.

The circles continue forever, so the remaining gap tends to zero and the exact total is

S = 1 square unit
Final distinction: The total red area is 1; the given blue area is 3. The complete coloured stack has area 4. Its one-quarter-size copy has area 1, which is precisely the red stack.

Final answer

The triangle’s 3 : 3 : 2 side ratio forces neighbouring radii to halve. Their areas are therefore multiplied by one-quarter. The given blue area of 3 generates a red area series whose exact sum is:

Total red area = 1 square unit

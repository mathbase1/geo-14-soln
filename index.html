A finite answer hiding inside infinity

Infinitely many circles.
How much red?

One blue circle, an endless red stack, and two geometric discoveries. The answer stays hidden until you reveal the proof.

The exact question

An isosceles triangle has side lengths in the ratio 3:3:2, with the equal sides corresponding to the two 3s. Its blue incircle has area 3 square units.

Above it, an infinite chain of red circles approaches the top vertex. Every red circle is tangent to both equal sides and to the circle immediately below it.

What is the total area of all the red circles?

What is—and is not—given

The side ratio fixes the triangle’s shape. The blue area fixes its scale. The circles touch but do not overlap.

No radius ratio is supplied. It must be proved. The red circles continue indefinitely; only the first few can be drawn visibly.

The ratios are lengths, not areas. The 3 inside the blue circle is an area, not its radius.
Hint 1 · Look at one right triangle

Bisect the top angle. Half the base divided by an equal side is one-third. What does that tell you about a circle’s radius and the distance from its centre to the top vertex?

Hint 2 · Describe one segment twice

The distance between two neighbouring centres is both the difference of their distances from the apex and the sum of their radii.

Hint 3 · Do not add the circles one at a time

Try shrinking the entire stack about the top vertex. What does it become?

The solution

Two discoveries.
One short equation.

Step 1 of 8

Read the question—not the drawing

The two equal sides and the base have ratio 3:3:2. Write them as 3k, 3k and 2k. Here k is an unknown positive length, not a measurement supplied by the question.

The blue circle is the incircle: it touches all three sides. Each red circle touches both sloping sides and the circle immediately below it. The chain continues without end towards the top vertex.

The one given areablue area=3 square units

We want the sum of the areas of all the red circles. Do not assume their radius ratio from how they look.

Step 2 of 8

One angle controls every circle

Draw the altitude AF from the top vertex to the base. In an isosceles triangle, it bisects both the base and the top angle.

So half the base is k, while the sloping side is 3k. Let the angle between the altitude and a sloping side be α.

sinα=oppositehypotenuse=FCAC=k3k=13

We never need the numerical angle. The useful fact is that its sine is one-third.

The 1 and 3 come from half the base and one equal side. They are not circle measurements.

Step 3 of 8

Prove where each centre must be

A circle touching both sloping sides has its centre equally far from those sides. Its centre therefore lies on the internal angle bisector AF.

Let O be the blue centre, let its radius be R, and let E be its contact point with the right side. A radius meets a tangent at a right angle, so OE is perpendicular to that side.

sinα=OEAO=RAO=13
AO=3R

For the first red circle, let P be its centre, r its radius, and D its contact point with the same sloping side. The same right-triangle reasoning gives:

AP=3r
A centre is three radii from the apex. This is a proved property of this triangle, not an extra given.
Step 4 of 8

Tangency reveals the hidden factor

The red centre P lies between the apex A and the blue centre O. Their separation can be described in two ways.

Subtract along the centre linePO=AO−AP=3R−3r
Add the touching radiiPO=R+r

Both expressions are the length of the same segment, so set them equal:

3R−3r=R+r
2R=4r
r=R2

The argument works for any neighbouring pair, since every circle touches the same two sides. Every successive radius is exactly half the preceding radius.

Step 5 of 8

Half the radius means a quarter of the area

The area of a circle is πR2. Halving its radius gives:

π(R2)2=14πR2

Every circle has one-quarter of the area of the circle below it. The first red circle therefore has area 34; the next has area 316; and the next has area 364.

Halving a radius does not halve an area. Area contains the square of the radius, so the scale factor is (12)2=14.
Step 6 of 8

Shrink the entire stack—not just one circle

Call the total red area S. The entire stack, including the blue circle, has area 3+S.

Now shrink every length by one-half, keeping the apex fixed. The blue circle moves onto the first red circle. The first red moves onto the second. Each remaining circle moves onto the next one.

The scaled copy of the entire stack is exactly the original red stack. Nothing is left over: every red circle has exactly one predecessor.

scaled whole-stack area=3+S4

Use the slider below the diagram to watch the one-to-one match. At its endpoint, the gold outlines coincide with the red circles.

Step 7 of 8

Where the final equation comes from

There is only one red region, but we now have two descriptions of its area.

Its namered total=S
The shrunk whole stackred total=3+S4

These are the same area. That is why:

S=3+S4

The 3 is the blue area given in the question. The 4 comes from the proved area scale factor. The S on the right is the original red total, which is part of the full stack.

4S=3+S
3S=3
S=1
All the red circles together1 square unit
Step 8 of 8

Why the infinite argument is legitimate

The circles do not overlap, and all lie inside a bounded triangle. Their total area is finite, so scaling and regrouping them is valid.

There is also a direct finite check. For the first N red circles, let the total be SN. The sum is:

SN=34+316+⋯+34N

Multiplying by four and subtracting the original finite sum leaves:

3SN=3−34N
SN=1−14N

The missing amount is 14N, which tends to zero. Thus the infinite total is exactly one square unit—not merely a rounded decimal.

Only finitely many circles can be shown on a screen. The diagram’s continuation mark represents the rest; the proof covers all of them.
